Homogenization run backwards

Averaging takes a fast variable and computes the slow equation it leaves behind. The construction decides the slow term it wants and builds the fast variable.

The homogenization pages carry a slow quantity and a fast hidden one, replace the fast one by its average, and compute what the average leaves out. The blow-up construction has the same skeleton, the same averaging operators, the same solvability conditions, and the same recursion to every order. It differs in direction. This page sets the two side by side.

One map, two directions. Forward is analysis: given the microstructure, what is the effective equation? Backward is design: given the effective term you need, which microstructure supplies it?

The reversal

On the homogenization pages the microstructure is data and the effective coefficient is the output. The forward map is well defined and, at each order, unique. In the construction the effective stress $T$ in the annulus is the target, fixed by the mismatch between core and exterior, and the microstructure is chosen to produce it. A backward map need not exist. The admissible stress cone condition of Section 4.3 is the statement that it does: the mean stresses two pulse families can produce form the cone $\{c_1v_1+c_2v_2: c_i\ge0\}$, and the core profiles are shaped so that $T$ lies strictly inside it. In the language of composites this is a G-closure question, which effective tensors a class of microstructures can reach, answered here by construction rather than characterized. The design step has a name too: Sigmund called the search for a microstructure with a prescribed effective tensor an inverse homogenization problem, and solved it by optimization over a unit cell. The pulse construction is an inverse homogenization solved by hand. Neither term is used in fluids, where the same objects go by Reynolds stress and the h-principle; the bridge between the vocabularies is this page's, not the literature's.

The solvability condition changes sides too. In homogenization it is a constraint one discovers: the corrector equation has a solution only when the source averages to zero, and that requirement is what produces the averaged equation. In the construction it is a design rule: anything with nonzero mean cannot be cancelled by a wave and must be routed to the mean-flow correction, and anything the mean-flow correction cannot absorb, the five radial moments, gets its own five-parameter family of bumps. Section 8 is a solvability argument written as an algorithm.

Two differences

The analogy is exact in structure and it breaks in two places, which between them explain the fluctuation pages.

The fast variable is endogenous. The switching chain does not know about the slow equation; it runs at its own rate $\lambda$. The pulses are created by the background shear and fed by it, and their carrier frequency $k\asymp\varepsilon^{-1/2}$ grows as the core collapses. The scale separation the averaging needs is enforced band by band: within a dyadic band the slow variables vary on a scale $S_*^{-3}$ and the carrier on $\varepsilon^{1/2}$. That is why the construction can keep averaging all the way to the singular time and why an exogenous switch of fixed rate cannot follow it. An endogenous fast scale keeps pace with $\tau$; an exogenous one is eventually slow.

The correction is leading order by design. On the homogenization pages the Green–Kubo term is $O(1/\lambda)$, a correction to an averaged answer that is already right. In the construction the pulse amplitude is tuned so that $A_{\rm wave}^2/\ell_{\rm wave}$ is exactly the scale of the mean residual, $q^{-3/2-h}$. The Reynolds stress is not a corrector to the mean equation; it is a term of the mean equation. This is the answer to the question that began this site. An averaged Navier–Stokes in the sense of dropping the fast variable is not the averaged equation of the construction, because it drops the term the construction exists to supply. The $1/\lambda$ corrector of an exogenous chain does not apply to oscillations the flow generates itself without a scaling theorem, and no such theorem is in the paper.

Parity, eddy viscosity, and the noise that undoes it

Dubrulle and Frisch showed that a parity-invariant small-scale flow homogenizes to an eddy viscosity: the Reynolds stress reduces to a diffusive term with a computable coefficient. Without parity invariance one gets a large-scale instability at first order instead. The pulses are chosen not to be parity invariant, and their mean stress is a directed flux aimed at a target, not a diffusion. The transport noise of Agresti runs the forward map: its scaling limit is an eddy viscosity with enhanced dissipation, which is the parity-invariant case. That is exactly the term the tuned balance cannot tolerate, and it is why the fluctuations page records that model as the one in which the singularity does not survive.

What this buys a reader

Someone who has worked through the two-state switch on the homogenization pages already knows the shape of Sections 3, 7, 8 and 9 of the paper: extend to a fast variable, average, impose solvability, solve the cell problem, recurse. What is new is the direction and the two differences above. The bookkeeping is heavier because every order matters and the expansion must be summed, but no step is of a kind the reader has not seen. The pulses page is the cell problem, the corrections page is the engine.
